Purpose and workload fit

Estimates where a slow climb that has already started is heading, from the newest stretch of history: the level is an EWMA, the slope is the median of the slopes between the points of the last few steps, and the slope is followed for fewer and fewer steps (damping), so the forecast bends towards a ceiling instead of being extrapolated without limit. It suits a load that creeps up with sudden drops mixed in (restarts, garbage collection), such as memory and caches. It needs no season and starts from a few dozen samples.

It cannot see a rise before it starts and does not know a daily cycle: a burst without warning needs capacity in reserve, and a recurring peak is for the seasonal algorithms (which need enough repeats of their cycle in the history before they may decide). It is a candidate: it must beat the baselines in a backtest before it is followed.

History and runtime requirements

At least window_steps observations (12 by default, 3 at the least).

Inputs must be regularly sampled, gap-free, and finite. Outputs retain the metric unit, such as CPU cores, memory bytes, or requests per second. The sample interval determines the duration of a step. Minimum history permits execution; it does not establish forecast quality.

This method does not use covariates; target metrics are forecast separately.

How it works and the formula

The damped trend answers "where does a slow climb that has begun go". The level is an EWMA, the slope is the median of the slopes between the points of the latest stretch, and the slope is followed for fewer and fewer steps, so the forecast bends towards a ceiling instead of running away.

level:     ℓ = the EWMA of the whole history (coefficient α, recursing from the first value)
slope:     b = the median of the slopes (y(j) − y(i)) / (j − i) over all pairs of points of the last window_steps points (Theil-Sen)
           if b < 0:  b ← b × down_share
forecast:  ŷ(T+h) = ℓ + b · (φ + φ² + … + φʰ)               φ = damping
           limited to ≤ cap_ratio × the highest value of the window, and not below 0

for φ < 1, however large h is, the forecast goes no further than  ℓ + b · φ / (1 − φ)

How the parameters enter the formula:

  • alpha: the EWMA coefficient of the level.
  • window_steps: how many steps the slope is taken from (default 12, also the least history). Shorter follows a rise that has just begun faster, and is also more exposed to noise.
  • damping (φ): the damping. The slope is multiplied by φ for each step ahead. At the default 0.9 the forecast goes at most to ℓ + 9·b; 1 means no damping, a straight line forever.
  • down_share: how much of a falling slope is followed. 0 (the default) is none: the forecast is flat when the load falls and does not hurry capacity down; whether to scale in is the decision's business and it has its own hold time.
  • cap_ratio: the forecast stays at most this times the highest value of the window, so a steep short slope is not extrapolated far.

Why it is done this way: memory and caches often "creep up slowly with sudden drops mixed in (restarts, garbage collection)". A line fitted to the whole history is bent by the drops; the level alone always lags the climb. The median slope is not moved by one or two isolated spikes or one sudden fall; the damping is there because the slope of a short window does not last forever, and letting the forecast converge to a ceiling is more honest than a straight line.

What it cannot do: it cannot see a burst before the rise has begun, nor a daily cycle. A burst with no warning needs capacity in reserve; a periodic peak needs a seasonal algorithm. It is a candidate and must beat the baseline in the backtest before it is adopted.

Parameter / typeDefault / range / unitMeaning and use
alpha
float		0.3
≥ 0.001; ≤ 1		EWMA coefficient of the level; larger values follow the newest value more closely. In (0, 1].
window_steps
int		12
≥ 3; ≤ 1000; steps		How many of the newest steps the slope is taken from. Shorter reacts faster to a rise that has just begun and follows more noise; convert to time with the step, e.g. 12 steps of 30 seconds is 6 minutes. It is also the minimum history of the algorithm.
damping
float		0.9
≥ 0.01; ≤ 1		Damping coefficient φ: the slope is multiplied by φ for every step further ahead, so the forecast reaches level + slope·φ/(1−φ) at most. 1 means no damping (a straight line that keeps rising); smaller converges sooner.
down_share
float		0
≥ 0; ≤ 1		The share of a falling slope that is followed: 0 = none (the forecast stays at the level, slow to give capacity back), 1 = all of it. When to scale down is the decision's business; the forecast does not hurry it.
cap_ratio
float		1.5
≥ 1; ≤ 10		The forecast stays at most this multiple of the highest value of the recent window, so a steep short slope is not extrapolated far.

Configuration example

Edit parameters in Algorithm management. This is a parameter object, not a complete config.yaml. The window assumes 30-second sampling; adjust for your actual interval. Omitted optional values use the defaults above.

{
  "alpha": 0.3,
  "window_steps": 12,
  "damping": 0.9,
  "down_share": 0,
  "cap_ratio": 1.5
}

Worked example: data, parameters and substitution

A memory (GiB) creeping up slowly, one point every 5 minutes, with two small dips mixed in. The parameters are the defaults except the window, shortened to 6 steps to keep the arithmetic short.

{
  "alpha": 0.3,
  "window_steps": 6,
  "damping": 0.9,
  "down_share": 0,
  "cap_ratio": 1.5
}
y = [50, 51, 53, 52, 55, 57, 56, 59]        n = 8

Step 1, the level ℓ (an EWMA of all 8 points, α = 0.3):

50 → 0.3×51+0.7×50 = 50.3 → 0.3×53+0.7×50.3 = 51.11 → 51.377 → 52.4639 → 53.8247 → 54.4773 → 0.3×59+0.7×54.4773 = 55.8341
ℓ = 55.8341

Step 2, the slope b (the median of the pairwise slopes of the last 6 points [53, 52, 55, 57, 56, 59]). 6 points make 15 pairs; compute (y(j)−y(i))/(j−i) for each and sort:

−1, −1, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1, 1.2, 1.3333, 1.3333, 1.3333, 1.75, 2, 2.5, 3, 3
the 8th (the median) = 1.3333
b = 1.3333 (per step)

Note that the negative slopes −1 from the dips in the window (53→52, 57→56) sit at the very front and do not move the median: that is "the median is not sensitive to isolated drops".

Step 3, substitute (φ = 0.9, the cumulative damped steps φ+φ²+…):

hφ + … + φʰŷ = ℓ + b × cumulative
10.955.8341 + 1.3333×0.9 = 57.0341
20.9 + 0.81 = 1.7155.8341 + 1.3333×1.71 = 58.1141
31.71 + 0.729 = 2.43955.8341 + 1.3333×2.439 = 59.0861

The result is [57.0341, 58.1141, 59.0861], each increment smaller than the one before (1.20, 1.08, 0.97). However far ahead it goes, it reaches 55.8341 + 1.3333 × 9 = 67.83 at most; the cap cap_ratio × the highest value of the window = 1.5 × 59 = 88.5, and here the damping acts first, so the cap is not hit.

Set damping to 1 (no damping) and the same data adds 1.3333 at every step: the forecast keeps rising until the cap of 88.5 stops it.

These numbers are computed by the real code in pkg/forecast/docs_examples_test.go and checked on every test run.

Tuning and tradeoffs

A shorter window catches a rise that has just begun sooner and is more easily misled by noise; the median slope ignores one or two isolated spikes or drops, but is unreliable when most points of the window are noisy. A smaller damping converges nearer and under-forecasts a climb that goes on; a damping near 1 goes far and is held by cap_ratio. With down_share at 0 the forecast is flat while the load falls, and when to scale down is decided by the hold and the cooldown of the decision.

Sources and references

  • Gardner, E.S. & McKenzie, E. "Forecasting trends in time series", Management Science 31(10), 1985, 1237–1246: the damped trend, the trend fading at every step so as not to overshoot.
  • Gardner, E.S. "Exponential smoothing: The state of the art—Part II", International Journal of Forecasting 22(4), 2006, 637–666: reviews the damped trend and recommends it as a robust default.
  • Theil (1950), Sen (1968): the slope as the median of pairwise slopes; see the peak envelope page.
  • This algorithm combines "an EWMA level + a median slope over a window + damping + following only part of a falling slope + a cap" for a load that creeps up with sudden drops; it is not the algorithm of one paper as it stands.

Use in a module

  1. Save parameters and check history, sample interval, and horizon.
  2. Enable module prediction. Fixed mode runs the selected method beside the other enabled ones and only the selected one decides; dynamic mode uses centrally enabled methods.
  3. Run rolling backtests and earn version promotion before adoption. Re-run backtests after changes affecting model identity.
  4. Review error, peak underestimation, runtime, and adoption reasons in Observe or Recommend before separately authorizing admission and replica actions.

For insufficient history, invalid settings, timeouts, or invalid output, inspect run reasons. Without eligible forecasts, the module follows its schedules, fallback, and protection rules.
