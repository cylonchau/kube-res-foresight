Purpose and workload fit

Uses a time-decayed historical percentile as a constant future level. Useful for comparing high-water resource sizing, without predicting when a peak occurs.

History and runtime requirements

At least four observations; include complete business cycles to represent typical peaks.

Inputs must be regularly sampled, gap-free, and finite. Outputs retain the metric unit, such as CPU cores, memory bytes, or requests per second. The sample interval determines the duration of a step. Minimum history permits execution; it does not establish forecast quality.

This method does not use covariates; target metrics are forecast separately.

How it works and the formula

The historical percentile does not model the future. It answers "for p% of the time the load did not exceed what?" and carries that value forward as a flat line. Newer samples weigh more: a sample half_life_hours older than the newest one weighs half as much.

half-life in steps:  H = half_life_hours × 3600 ÷ step seconds
sample weight:       w(i) = 0.5 ^ (age(i) / H), age(i) = steps before the newest sample (the newest has age 0, weight 1)
percentile value Q:  sort the samples by value, add up the weights, take the first value whose cumulative weight ≥ (percentile/100) × total weight
forecast:            ŷ(T+h) = Q × (1 + safety_margin)          h = 1 … H (a flat line)

How the parameters enter the formula:

  • percentile (1 to 100): which percentile. 95 means "for 95% of the (weighted) time the load was not above this", the larger the more conservative.
  • half_life_hours: the half-life. Shorter looks only at the recent past, longer approaches the plain percentile of everything; 0 means no decay.
  • safety_margin: multiply by 1+margin as extra room. 0.1 keeps 10% more.

Why it is done this way: the Kubernetes VPA and Google's Autopilot size resource requests this way: with a decayed percentile, not a mean. A mean leaves the workload short half of the time; a high percentile covers most peaks, and the decay lets it follow a rising load quickly and forget a past peak slowly. For a memory recommendation where "better to give more than to OOM" holds, it is the usual comparison.

What it cannot do: it is a flat line: it does not model the future, knows no cycle and does not know when a peak will arrive. It is a deliberately high forecast, not the expected load, which is why the need breakdown of the platform labels it P90 or P95.

  "safety_margin": 0.1
}

Worked example: data, parameters and substitution

The last 8 samples (one every 5 minutes) of a memory usage in MiB, with one short peak of 60. Parameters: 90th percentile, half-life 0.5 hours (= 30 minutes = 6 steps), 10% margin on top.

  "half_life_hours": 0.5,

The cumulative weight first reaches ≥ 4.9755 at the last cell (at 46 it is only 4.8212, just short), so Q = 60. That is what a high percentile means: the peak holds only 12.8% of the weight, but the 90th percentile must cover 90% of the weight, so the peak is included.

Step 3, add the margin: ŷ = 60 × (1 + 0.1) = 66. The forecast is [66, 66] (2 steps).

For comparison: with percentile 50 (the median) the first value whose cumulative weight is ≥ 2.7642 is 44, so the forecast is 44 before the 10% margin; the gap of 16 MiB between the high percentile and the median is the price of being conservative. With half_life_hours 0 (no decay) the 90th percentile of the same data is still 60, because the peak is 1 of 8 points and the 90% target has to cover it.

These numbers are computed by the real code in pkg/forecast/docs_examples_test.go and checked on every test run.

Tuning and tradeoffs

percentile uses a 1–100 scale, such as 95. A shorter half-life emphasizes recent values; zero disables decay. safety_margin multiplies the result by 1+margin; evaluate compounded headroom.

Sources and references

  • The recommender of the Kubernetes Vertical Pod Autoscaler (VPA): a histogram with exponential decay (a half-life), a target percentile and a safety margin. Source and design documents: the vertical-pod-autoscaler directory of kubernetes/autoscaler.
  • Rzadca, K. et al. "Autopilot: Workload Autoscaling at Google", EuroSys 2020: Google's Autopilot also uses decayed histograms and percentiles, and compares several recommenders.
  • Difference from the VPA: here the weighted percentile is taken directly over the sample series (no buckets), and the defaults are the 95th percentile with a 24-hour half-life; they are not the defaults of the VPA, do not copy them over.

Use in a module

  1. Save parameters and check history, sample interval, and horizon.
  2. Enable module prediction. Fixed mode runs the selected method; dynamic mode uses centrally enabled methods.
  3. Run rolling backtests and earn version promotion before adoption. Re-run backtests after changes affecting model identity.
  4. Review error, peak underestimation, runtime, and adoption reasons in Observe or Recommend before separately authorizing admission and replica actions.

For insufficient history, invalid settings, timeouts, or invalid output, inspect run reasons. Without eligible forecasts, the module follows its schedules, fallback, and protection rules.
